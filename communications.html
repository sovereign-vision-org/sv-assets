What This Network Does

Six pillars of
sovereign connection.

Te Puka Loloa is more than an antenna. It is a commitment — to connection, to safety, to self-determination. Here is what every node delivers to the people of Tuvalu.

1
Feso'ota'i
Sovereign Connection
LTE · Wi-Fi · VoIP · No foreign carrier

Every family, school, clinic, and business within range can make calls, send messages, and access the internet — using their existing phone. The Open5GS core runs entirely on-island hardware, serving up to 500 concurrent subscribers per node.

  • Private LTE core — Open5GS EPC on Helix 330
  • FreeSWITCH SIP gateway — on-island VoIP
  • Dual-band Wi-Fi 6 — indoor and outdoor coverage
  • Steward-administered SIM provisioning
"This is what connects the island — so everyone can talk, learn, and reach the world on their own terms."
2
Puipuiga
Emergency Lifeline
Works when everything else fails

When a cyclone hits, this node stays operational. It runs on battery for 24+ hours at critical load with no solar input. The VHF/UHF emergency radio cannot be switched off remotely. Steward-authored broadcast texts activate automatically on pre-defined storm triggers.

  • 14 kWh LiFePO₄ — 24+ hours critical autonomy
  • VHF/UHF dual-band — Icom + Comet GP-3
  • Storm protection mode — auto-activates at 90 kph
  • Mast retracts to 2.3m — steward-operated winch
"Even during a cyclone, this node keeps communication alive — and the human decides everything."
3
Nofoaga Lautele
Guardian Intelligence
Observes and alerts — never decides

Two NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX modules run the Local Guardian edge AI — monitoring 80+ sensor feeds, detecting degradation trends weeks before failure, and guiding stewards through photo-assisted diagnostics. All image processing is local. No data leaves the node.

  • 2× Jetson Orin NX 16GB — local inference only
  • Photo diagnostics — offline vision model
  • 22" Guardian Terminal — IP54, glove-compatible
  • Tuvaluan and English — steward selects at login
"The system does not replace the operator. It ensures the operator succeeds."
4
Mata'itu
Civic Data Backbone
Water · energy · food · all connected

Every node is the nervous system for the wider TSV infrastructure. Sensor telemetry from Vai Tapu desalination, ArborMesh solar, and Te Fenua Fakafoou waste systems flows through the mesh to Guardian Core. The network makes the whole system intelligent.

  • MQTT broker — Mosquitto on Helix 330 Node 2
  • 10 VLAN zones — fully isolated by function
  • Guardian presents cross-system analytics
  • Audit log — append-only, community council access
"Communications is not a service. It is the connective tissue that makes sovereignty possible."
5
Tuto'atasi
Data Sovereignty
No foreign cloud. No foreign AI. No exceptions.

All subscriber records, Guardian inference, sensor logs, and camera footage remain on local hardware under Tuvaluan governance. LUKS2 encryption on all NVMe volumes. Keys held by stewards — not by Guardian. DeepSeek and all foreign-hosted AI models are excluded by architecture.

  • Dual pfSense — CARP failover, steward-managed rules
  • WireGuard VPN — only remote access path
  • Mutual TLS on all Guardian API calls
  • VLAN 99 quarantine — unknown devices dropped
"Autonomy preserves function. Stewardship preserves authority."
6
Te Kaukau o te Atamai
Youth Stewardship
Maintenance is a generation — not a contract

Each node is maintained by locally trained stewards who hold the civic oath, the steward vest, and the node's glyph. Youth trainees progress through a structured 12-month programme — from safety and power basics to CompTIA certification and Guardian Developer Path.

  • Minimum 2 stewards per node — civic roles
  • Naming ceremony activates each node
  • Community cinema nights — fortnightly civic use
  • Steward garden — solar canopy rainwater catchment
"Infrastructure that a community names and tends is infrastructure that endures."

System Architecture Overview
The Te Puka Loloa architecture is organised in four tiers: Backhaul (satellite, fiber, relay), Edge Nodes (compute, firewall, LTE core), Access Layer (users, civic sites, sensors), and Power System. Each tier operates independently — failure of any tier does not disable the tiers below it.
All tiers active · Guardian monitoring nominal

Power Constraint
LTE · Emergency comms · Guardian preserved. Guest Wi-Fi, secondary AC shed at SOC <35%.

Physical Infrastructure · Per Node

Every node is
a complete station.

A single Te Puka Loloa node is a self-contained sovereign communications platform — solar-powered, cyclone-rated, and deployable by a four-person team in five to seven days. Everything from LTE radio to edge AI to rainwater collection is integrated into one skid-mounted shelter.

Solar Canopy · 6.4 kW
16× 400W monocrystalline panels on engineered roof supports. Gutters channel rainwater to the steward's garden cistern. Dual Victron MPPT 250/100 controllers in redundant configuration — each maintains charging independently if the other fails.
Communications Mast · SMC STORM ST5-12
12 m retractable mast with pole-mount base plate and cable tray. Three 120° LTE sector antennas, GigaBeam wireless bridge radio, and Wi-Fi access point for local mesh. Stows to 2.3 m on Guardian storm advisory — steward operates the winch.
Guardian Terminal · 22" IP54 Kiosk
Capacitive touchscreen HMI rated for use with work gloves. HID Signo 40 NFC + PIN authentication. Operates in Tuvaluan and English. Every Guardian recommendation, alert, and preservation action is visible here — the human authority point for the entire node.
Infrastructure Shelter · 10 m × 6 m
Fiberglass construction, corrosion-resistant and salt-spray rated. R-19 insulation throughout. Skid base with M16 anchor eyelets for crane or flatbed delivery. Double access doors, ventilation louvers on all panels, and dual Daikin 12,000 BTU mini-split cooling.
Power Entry Panel · Victron MultiPlus-II 48V
Pure sine wave hybrid inverter at 94% efficiency. Feeds the entire 42U rack, dual HVAC units, Guardian Terminal, and lighting from one integrated panel. Cerbo GX publishes SOC, PV yield, and battery temperature to Guardian via MQTT — observable, never autonomous.
Rainwater Collection · Triple-tank cascade
Solar canopy gutters channel rainwater into a triple-tank cascade system beside the shelter. Irrigates the steward's garden — a civic gathering space used for community cinema nights and cultural events. Infrastructure designed to give back to the place it serves.

Lumana'i · Deployment Roadmap

Phased across
the archipelago.

Beginning in Funafuti's civic core, the network grows outward — reaching every outer island and eventually integrating with the Vaka undersea cable. Phase 1 hardware is pre-wired for every future upgrade.

01
Phase 1 · Now · Funafuti Core
10 Nodes — Critical and Civic Sites

Airport, Wharf, Government Precinct, Princess Margaret Hospital, Vai Tapu Desalination Hub, South Funafuti Coast, Funafala School Cluster, RRN civic node, Sports Oval, and North Funafuti residential. Private LTE core launches. Youth stewards trained and certified. Guardian Edge begins monitoring-only operation. Total: ~$1.08–1.52M.

02
Phase 2 · Outer Islands
Archipelago-Wide Expansion

All eight outer atolls receive nodes using the same hardware with extended battery banks. Lower steward visit frequency places greater reliance on Guardian's predictive maintenance capability. Outer island stewards complete a more comprehensive self-diagnosis module. Satellite transitions to primary backhaul in remote locations.

03
Phase 3 · Fibre Integration
Vaka Cable — Global Connection

Every shelter includes a pre-wired fibre-ready port. When the Vaka Cable (Google Bulikula system, AIFFP-funded) lands, each node connects with no hardware changes. Satellite transitions to resilience-only backup. Cutover is steward-initiated via Guardian Terminal — Guardian presents that fibre is available; the steward confirms the switch.

Te Kaukau o te Atamai

Named, sworn,
and tended.

Cultural integration is a maintenance strategy — not a communication strategy. Infrastructure that a community names, tends, and feels ownership of is maintained with greater diligence, defended against neglect, and inspires the next generation to seek steward roles.

The naming ceremony, the civic oath, the steward glyph, and the youth training programme are load-bearing elements of the long-term operational model.

Naming Ceremony
Each node receives its identity
Community names the node from ancestral navigators, coral formations, or prevailing winds. The name logs to Guardian Core registry at the moment of ceremony. From that moment, every log entry, every terminal screen, and every engineering document uses the node's name — not a hardware ID.
Civic Oath
The steward commits publicly
Each assigned steward reads the civic oath at the naming ceremony — a commitment to maintain the node with care, to serve community access as a right, and to pass knowledge to the next generation. The oath is recorded in the Guardian Terminal audit log.
Youth Training · 12 months
Stewardship is a career path
Trainees progress through safety and power basics (Phase 1), two hands-on specialisations (Phase 2), and CompTIA / Victron Academy certification (Phase 3). Graduating stewards receive the cloak pin, steward certificate, and a public naming ceremony with their node.
Community Cinema · Fortnightly
The node as a civic place
Stewards organise fortnightly outdoor cinema nights using the node's edge compute, locally cached content, projector output, and outdoor speakers. The steward's garden — irrigated by solar canopy rainwater catchment — provides a civic gathering space adjacent to the node.
Category 5 Storm Protocol · Planned Shutdown

Survival first.
Restart fast.

The 150 MPH shelter rating applies when the node is in stowed configuration — mast retracted, canopy folded, panels sealed. For Category 4–5 events, stewards execute a deliberate planned shutdown before the storm arrives. This is not a failure mode. It is the intended protocol.

"The node that weathers a Category 5 intact and restores service within hours is more resilient than one that attempts to operate through the storm and is destroyed."

Wind approaching 90 kph
Automatic alert — no steward action required

  • Wind trend displayed from onsite weather station
  • Steward decides when preparation begins
  • Sensor polling increases to 30-second intervals
  • No automatic actions taken

Phase 2 · Physical Preparation
6–24 hours before arrival
Steward-initiated · Guardian guides each step
  • Lower mast to 2.3 m using winch · lock and clamp
  • Fold solar canopy flat · hinge mechanism · ~10 min
  • Secure or remove all external equipment
  • Close and lock all shelter access panels
  • Inspect anchor bolts and tie-downs
  • Charge battery bank to ≥90% SOC
3
Phase 3 · Controlled Shutdown
Storm imminent
Steward-confirmed at each stage
  • Notify LTE subscribers via SMS broadcast
  • Suspend guest Wi-Fi, then LTE radio stack
  • Guardian logs final telemetry snapshot
  • Suspend HVAC — shelter relies on thermal mass
  • Suspend inverter · isolate DC from AC loads
  • System enters sealed dormant state
4
Phase 4 · Post-Storm Restart
After storm passes
Steward-initiated · Guardian runs checklist
  • Physical inspection of shelter, anchors, mast, canopy
  • Guardian Terminal boots · sensor baseline check
  • Raise mast · inspect before energising
  • Restore inverter · Guardian presents restoration order
  • Restore LTE core · configuration integrity check
  • Full service restoration · typically 2–4 hours

Wind Rating — Engineering Context

The 150 MPH (241 km/h) shelter design rating applies in stowed configuration: mast at 2.3 m, canopy folded flat, all panels sealed. This exceeds the sustained wind speed of every cyclone that has made direct landfall in Funafuti on record. The South Pacific basin has produced gusts exceeding 300 km/h in extreme events — Cyclone Zoe (2002) peaked at 285 km/h sustained; Cyclone Pam (2015) produced a state of emergency in Tuvalu at 250 km/h. The protocol above is the engineered response to that reality. The solar canopy folding mechanism is a required component for Tuvalu deployment.
